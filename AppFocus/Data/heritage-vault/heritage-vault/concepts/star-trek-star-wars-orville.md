# Star Trek, Star Wars, The Orville

Three related but distinct entries in the science-fiction / space-opera register. Lifelong fan relationships with all three.

## Star Trek

Lifelong fan. **Specifically: The Original Series (ToS), The Next Generation (TNG), and Deep Space Nine (DS9).** The three series that share the humanist-optimism register, the episodic-moral-argument format, and the found-family crew structure.

The selection is diagnostic. **Voyager** — also liked, with a specific observation: *"Kate Mulgrew shares Katharine Hepburn's flair for the dramatic. The show was basically Gilligan's Island."* Both halves accurate. Mulgrew's performance brings Hepburn-quality dignity-under-pressure and theatrical command to Janeway — the [[visual-author-loyalty]] attention operating on acting, watching the performer's specific gift rather than the property around them. The show itself consistently failed to commit to consequence (every episode: could get home, something prevents it, reset) — precisely the failure mode that makes DS9 by contrast remarkable. Liked for Mulgrew; accurately characterized for what it was. ToS/TNG/DS9 represents **the classic trek sensibility at its most developed**: diverse crew, philosophical problems explored through metaphor, the outsider (Spock, Data, Odo) as the lens on humanity.

**DS9 specifically** is worth marking as the most tonally complex entry. The most willing to let the Federation be wrong, to sustain moral ambiguity across seasons, to let characters be genuinely compromised by war and power. The closest Trek has come to long-arc consequence worldbuilding — closer to Claremont or GRRM than the episodic-reset format of earlier Trek. Choosing DS9 alongside ToS and TNG says something specific about the register: the moral seriousness matters as much as the humanist optimism.

**The Orville** (Seth MacFarlane, 2017–present) carries the classic-trek aesthetic forward more faithfully than the modern revivals. Same humanist-crew-as-found-family register, same optimism about humanity's future potential, genuine dramatic weight under the comedic surface. Essentially a love letter to what ToS/TNG were.

## Star Wars

Lifelong fan. **Specifically: the original trilogy.** The formation is the formation; the Prequels and Sequels exist at varying distances from it.

**X-Wing vs. TIE Fighter at Sony** — a small but real heritage detail. The 1997 multiplayer space combat simulator was part of the social fabric at [[Sony San Diego]] — a game played *together with colleagues*, probably during downtime or after hours. Part of the Scharmen-and-cohort social world. Not a solo formation experience but a shared one, the kind of game that builds collegial culture across a team.

## What these add together

The Trek/Wars pairing covers **the two poles of American sci-fi culture** — Star Wars (myth, feel, romance, the Force as spiritual system) and Star Trek (ideas, humanism, character, the Federation as moral project). Being a serious fan of both rather than tribal about either is consistent with the [[comparative-pluralist-method]] disposition: multiple coherent frameworks, each illuminating differently, none absolute.

DS9 specifically sits at the intersection: humanist optimism *tested* by sustained moral complexity, which is the most interesting position on the Trek-Wars axis — not naive optimism, not cynical myth, but *the humanist project under pressure*.

## What these add to the map

The science-fiction register on the literature axis is already populated: Heinlein (engineering-rationalism), Herbert (interlocking-systems-ecology), Moorcock (multiverse/cosmic). The screen SF here adds:

- **Humanist optimism about collective futures** (Trek) — a register not present elsewhere on the map, and one that probably informs the *Sentient Machines* enthusiasm for robotics as a field that matters
- **Mythological operatic scope** (Star Wars) — complements the Tolkien and Moorcock cosmic registers on the literature axis
- **Found-family-in-space** (both, Orville) — the recurring found-family-of-misfits pattern expressed in science fiction
