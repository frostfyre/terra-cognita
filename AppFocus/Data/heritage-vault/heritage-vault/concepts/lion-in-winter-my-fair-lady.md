# Film — The Lion in Winter and My Fair Lady

Two specific film entries that belong on their own rather than in a general cluster.

## The Lion in Winter (1968, Anthony Harvey)

**Annual viewing** — Paul watches this every year. That's its own tier marker: not a film seen and loved but a film returned to annually as a *calibration ritual*. Same function as [[Winsor McCay|Rarebit Fiend]] sought-out-on-multiple-occasions: a work that keeps giving something you need.

Peter O'Toole as Henry II, **[[Katharine Hepburn]]** as Eleanor of Aquitaine, Anthony Hopkins and Timothy Dalton in early roles, James Goldman's screenplay.

**What it gives:** verbal combat as primary action. A family that is simultaneously genuinely loving and genuinely trying to destroy each other. Historical setting (12th century Plantagenet) used to examine timeless power dynamics — inheritance, love, betrayal, the long game. An ensemble where everyone plays at full intensity without anyone dominating wrongly. It's a chamber piece that refuses to simplify any of its characters — Henry and Eleanor are both right and both wrong simultaneously for the entire film.

**The Hepburn thread:** The [[Joss Whedon|Voyager]] observation about Kate Mulgrew and Katharine Hepburn's flair for the dramatic now has its primary source. Eleanor of Aquitaine is *the* Hepburn role — theatrical intelligence under sustained pressure, dignity that doesn't condescend to the material, a character who is simultaneously the most imprisoned person in the room and its most powerful. Mulgrew as Janeway is that quality applied to Trek; it traces back here.

**The SCA connection:** Paul was involved in medieval recreation through the [[Society for Creative Anachronism]]. The Lion in Winter is one of the great dramatizations of medieval political reality — not costume-drama medievalism but the actual dynamics of 12th century Plantagenet power, the feudal inheritance system as dramatic engine. The annual return to this film alongside SCA participation suggests a real and sustained interest in the medieval world as a *living dynamic* rather than as spectacle.

**The operatic-tragic register:** The Lion in Winter is a film where everyone wants to win and everyone loses. The structure is pure [[operatic-tragic-register]] — the doomed cycle, the protagonist-as-problem (Henry), the antagonist-as-protagonist-who-is-also-right (Eleanor). No one gets what they want; the watching is the thing. Same structural register as Elric, Dark Phoenix, Tony Soprano.

## My Fair Lady (1964, George Cukor)

Named for a specific structural reason: **Rex Harrison's physical performance of Higgins directly informed Bugs Bunny's gesture vocabulary.**

Jones cited Shaw/My Fair Lady as formative for the Bugs/Elmer dynamic structurally — but the claim is more specific than structure. **Bugs's gestures, smirk, raised eyebrow, and timing of the turn come directly from Rex Harrison's specific embodiment of Higgins.** The carrot is the walking stick. The lean before a withering line is identical. The smirk timing — just before the victim realizes they've been insulted — matches precisely.

The lineage: **Rex Harrison's specific physical performance → Jones's observation of it → Bugs Bunny's gesture vocabulary.** A specific performer's hand informing another medium — Jones watching Harrison the way Paul watches Wendy Pini's line or Sanders hiding himself in a Mulan frame: absorbing the physical intelligence of a specific artist and translating it across media.

Watching My Fair Lady with awareness of this lineage is watching both the film *and* the source of something already loved downstream. **Bugs is theatrical where Daffy is reactive because Bugs is Harrison.** Daffy is id; Bugs is craft.

**The Pygmalion/role-authoring structure:** Eliza refuses to remain the thing Higgins made her, eventually claiming her own identity against the person who constructed her. The protagonist who outgrows the institution that formed her. Same arc as the Blizzard chapter ending, Buffy operating outside official authority, Joan Jett post-label-rejections. The [[role-authoring]] disposition applied to character arc.
