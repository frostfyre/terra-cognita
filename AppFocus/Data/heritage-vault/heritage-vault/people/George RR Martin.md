# George R.R. Martin

Two distinct bodies of work absorbed at different periods:

**Wild Cards** (shared-world anthology series, begun 1987) — read years before Perpetual, placing it in the bridge or early-career era.

***A Song of Ice and Fire*** (*A Game of Thrones* 1996, *A Clash of Kings* 1998, *A Storm of Swords* 2000, *A Feast for Crows* 2005, *A Dance with Dragons* 2011 — *The Winds of Winter* still unfinished as of 2025) — **read at [[Perpetual Entertainment]]**, roughly 2004-2007.

Reading order matters: Wild Cards first, then the solo epic. The collaborative worldbuilding project before the singular authorial vision.

## Wild Cards

GRRM's shared-world anthology — an alternate history where an alien virus released over New York in 1946 gives some people superpowers (Aces), horribly mutates others (Jokers), and kills most who are exposed. GRRM conceived and edits it; multiple writers contribute connected stories building a shared world.

**The shared-world anthology format** connects directly to [[Robert Asprin]]'s Thieves' World series — already on the map — which was the explicit model GRRM used when creating Wild Cards. Paul absorbed both ends of the same lineage: Asprin's Thieves' World and GRRM's Wild Cards, both shared-world anthologies built through collaborative contribution.

**The superhero-as-social-metaphor register** — Jokers as the marginalized, Aces as the privileged, government persecution and registration issues — is the same [[Chris Claremont|Claremont X-Men]] instinct Paul absorbed in comics form, now in prose anthology form. Multiple authors, same underlying political position: the person with powers as a lens on how society treats difference.

**The collaborative worldbuilding structure** — multiple writers, coherent shared world, connected but independent stories — is the literary version of the collaborative production environments Paul has worked in throughout his career.

## What the books specifically add

**Long-arc consequence worldbuilding in prose form** — the Claremont X-Men instinct applied to epic fantasy at novel length. Characters die and stay dead; political decisions have cascading consequences chapters and books later; the world changes and doesn't reset. Same structural commitment as Elfquest's long arc and DS9's sustained moral complexity, but at a scale that requires years of reading to inhabit.

**Historical research depth** — the Wars of the Roses as primary template, medieval economics and political structures taken seriously, the logistics of armies and sieges and succession as genuine narrative engines rather than backdrop. Same anthropological-seriousness instinct as Le Guin's worldbuilding — the internal logic of the world is real and consistent and has consequences.

**Perspective-chapter unreliability** — every POV character is partially wrong about what's happening. The reader assembles the truth from partial and biased accounts. That's a specific and sophisticated narrative technique that rewards sustained, attentive reading — you have to track what each character knows vs. what the reader knows vs. what's actually true.

**The [[protagonist-as-the-problem]] pattern** at civilizational scale — not one protagonist but a system of protagonists whose individual rationalities collectively produce catastrophe. The tragedy isn't that the good characters fail; it's that the system itself produces outcomes no one intended or wanted. The most political version of the pattern on the map, alongside Orwell's Animal Farm.
